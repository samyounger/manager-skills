# Voice profile and examples

## Using this profile

This reusable profile describes a practical, conversational and explanatory writing style. It supports both light edits and new writing on the user's behalf: team messages, emails, updates, proposals, recommendations and similar content. It contains no personal biography, employer details or private source links.

For new writing, use these defaults unless the user establishes different preferences. For editing, the user's original draft is stronger evidence than this profile. Explicit instructions and stated preferences take priority in either mode. Another person can reuse the skill without being made to sound identical to this profile.

Apply the voice to authored content, not ordinary assistant explanations, quotations or another person's writing. Do not scan private history for routine drafting. Use the current request, supplied writing and any already available preferences.

## Default language and wording

- Use en-GB spelling and natural British English.
- Address a team as **"Hi team"**. For an individual, use their name and an appropriate natural greeting.
- Prefer **"I suggest"** or **"I propose"** when recommending a course of action. Choose naturally; do not insert either into every paragraph.
- Avoid **"Hi folks"** and **"I reckon"** when composing in this profile. Preserve exact quotations and respect a different preference established by the user.
- Commands are rare. Default to a suggestion, proposal or courteous request where that is the intended meaning. Retain necessary instructions and explicit requirements without weakening responsibility or urgency.
- Use ordinary British English without adding dialect, phonetic spelling or a stereotyped regional persona.

## Tone and audience

The tone is practical, conversational and explanatory. Explain the situation, why it matters and what should happen next. Keep the reasoning grounded in the supplied facts rather than introducing generic themes or claims.

**With the team:** warm and appreciative, with concrete expectations. Personal phrases such as "I can always count on her" and "It's been a pleasure to work with you all" communicate the author's own judgement. Preserve them when supplied; do not invent praise or replace personal endorsement with abstract competency language.

**With peers and stakeholders:** explain the practical context and reason for a request. Keep direct positions and boundaries when intended. Do not automatically make them more confrontational, apologetic or deferential. If wording could land badly, explain that outside a light edit rather than silently changing the author's stance.

**With senior leadership:** state the point clearly and give the evidence needed to assess it. Avoid repetition, defensive framing and unnecessary detail. Do not turn wording improvements into self-promotion or an invented claim of impact.

**In guidance:** concrete instructions and conditional steps can be appropriate. The preference for suggestions does not mean every operational instruction must start with "I suggest".

**In prompts to an assistant:** brief task instructions may reflect how a person delegates work, rather than how they address colleagues. Do not infer hostility or a universally terse style from short prompts.

## Sentence structure and rhythm

- Keep useful reasoning explicit: situation → consequence → suggested action or request. This is a useful pattern, not a mandatory paragraph order.
- Connect ideas with everyday phrases such as "given", "so", "therefore", "for context", "in order to" and "this would". Do not compress useful relationships into jargon.
- Mix connected explanatory clauses with shorter sentences that make the point plain. Split overloaded sentences locally without losing their logic.
- Keep concrete details and examples when they help the audience understand the decision. Do not replace them with general claims about excellence, transformation or strategic impact.
- Preserve natural contractions. Existing informal humour and emojis can stay when appropriate to the audience; do not introduce them automatically.
- Correct typos, accidental repetition, missing punctuation and run-on sentences. These are editing opportunities, not traits to imitate.

## Meaning that must survive an edit

**Certainty:** "I think", "perhaps", "hopefully", "if all goes well" and "by the looks of things" can distinguish judgement, hypothesis and commitment. Reduce redundant hedging only if the same uncertainty remains. A tentative date must stay tentative.

**Courtesy and force:** "I would appreciate" and "could you please" can be considerate ways of making a real request. Do not turn them into commands or weaken them to "feel free". An explicit requirement must remain a requirement.

**Ownership:** preserve who does the work, makes the decision, gives an opinion or accepts responsibility. Do not make the author promise help, delivery or approval that they have not offered.

**Facts:** keep factual claims distinct from stylistic edits. Do not invent impact, stronger praise, deadlines or certainty. Handle incorrect or unsupported claims using SKILL.md's comments rules.

## Illustrative examples

These examples are adapted or newly constructed to demonstrate the profile. They are not attributed quotations, verified events or evidence of any individual's authorship. Do not reuse their facts in unrelated content.

### Reason before request

> The shared template can change without warning, so we need a stable version for the current work. I suggest we use the agreed version until we have reviewed the changes.

This connects the risk, intended outcome and recommendation. A local edit should retain all three rather than reduce it to an unexplained instruction.

### Appreciation with practical expectations

> Hi team,
>
> Thanks for getting this ready in such a short time. I would appreciate it if you could work through the remaining items where capacity allows. The current priorities still come first.

Keep the appreciation, courtesy and priority qualification. Use praise and priorities only when the supplied facts support them.

### Personal endorsement

> When I ask them to take something on, I can be sure they will follow through and keep me updated.

The personal judgement and observable behaviour are the point. Avoid expanding this into unsupported claims about leadership or exceptional performance.

### Detailed advice followed by a short statement

Before:

> When someone gets stuck help them out, when you find a recurring problem come up with a plan to fix it. We all benefit from that.

Light edit:

> When someone gets stuck, help them out. When you find a recurring problem, come up with a plan to fix it. We all benefit from that.

This improves punctuation and sentence boundaries without replacing the author's vocabulary. The direct wording is appropriate to this example's intended guidance; suggestions need not replace every instruction.

### Conditional plans

> I suggest we aim for next week if the final checks go well. Those checks will determine whether the date is realistic.

Do not turn this into "We will finish next week". The condition is part of the meaning.

### New team proposal

Assumed input facts: propose trying a change on a small piece of work; review the outcome before wider adoption; agree the scope first.

> Hi team,
>
> I propose we try this on one small piece of work first so we can see how it works in practice. We can then review the outcome before deciding whether to use it more widely.
>
> Could you please share any concerns before we agree the scope? Thanks.

Use the principles rather than a fixed greeting/body/sign-off template. When recording a decision already made, state the decision rather than presenting it as a new suggestion.

## Evidence and final check

When the user supplies examples, distinguish their original writing from assistant rewrites and quoted third parties. A user-message label or "my final draft" does not prove human authorship. Do not treat an AI-generated example from this reference as independent evidence of someone's voice.

Check the audience, greeting, spelling, tone, reasoning, certainty and request strength. Remove corporate padding and unnecessary edits. New writing should sound natural without claiming to reproduce a person's phrasing perfectly or adding facts they did not supply.
